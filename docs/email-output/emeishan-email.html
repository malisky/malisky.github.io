October 30, 2025: Mount Emei
October 30, 2025: Mount Emei - Michael Alisky

It's 3am, and my alarm goes off, but I've been up for over an hour already, too excited to sleep. I scroll on my phone, fanning through a book called To Live. I roll out of bed, throw on my clothes, and begin the final ascent of Mount Emei in the darkness. I reach the Golden Summit just before sunrise.

Mount Emei, or Emeishan, is the highest of China's four sacred Buddhist mountains. Sandwiched between two layers of clouds sits a 660-ton gold-plated statue of the Indian bodhisattva Puxian, built in 2006. Legend has it that Puxian climbed this mountain on the back of a six-tusked white elephant and found enlightenment during the sixth century.

A sea of fog obscures the sweeping Chengdu Plain, and to the west and northeast, snowcapped mountains poke out like shark fins made of jade and pink marble. I rest for a while, call my parents, and take a few obligatory selfies with some Chinese uncles.

I started my hike yesterday before dawn at Baoguo Temple, and 12 hours and 8,000 feet of elevation gain later, I reached the Lei Dongping plateau as the sky turned dark. I spent the night in a monastery where I met Xue Ren, a blithesome monk who gave me a beer to go with some (non-vegetarian) instant noodles.

Any time Xue Ren used a word which he also knew in English, he would excitedly ask me if I knew what it meant. Bangongshi. That means office! he'd exclaim, followed by peals of laughter.

Xue Ren offering me a beer might have been a spiritual test, in which case I failed, but regardless, I was genuinely grateful to have a bed to sleep in and a chance to dry my rain-and-sweat soaked clothes. I fell asleep as soon as my head hit the pillow.

I walked alone for most of the 22-mile hike, but I did meet a group of runners from Chengdu who shared some potatoes with me. Temples along the way also offered free tea and hot water, while rest stations every few kilometers sold snacks and energy drinks. I thought about China's growing temple economy and the entrepreneurial spirit underlying spirituality, from temple-cum-hotels in Sichuan to Kung Fu training centers in Shaolin.

I also ran into several troops of fierce Tibetan macaques. Not to slander our simian ancestors, but these monkeys were honestly quite rude, getting way too close for comfort and eyeing the contents of my backpack rather expectantly.

As I climbed through the mist, I thought about the hundreds of thousands of pilgrims who, for over a millennium, have made this same journey, scaling the same narrow, never-ending set of steep stone stairs.

As Ian Johnson writes:

From places that themselves deserved worship, [China's mountains] became abodes of the immortals, or the sites of Buddhist and Daoist temples. They were places of serious contemplation. As the fourth-century Daoist Ge Hong put it, Never enter lightly into the mountains.

The Souls of China: The Return of Religion After Mao (p. 182)

It didn't seem, though, like any of the people I met on the trail were there seeking religious fulfillment. Most were avid hikers or college students who told me they were simply looking to challenge themselves. One man from Leshan had recently overcome a major illness, and this was his way of celebrating.

After leaving the Golden Summit, I worked my way down through throngs of tourists who had just arrived via bus and cable car. Yesterday's rain gave way to brilliantly clear skies, and I felt a sense of accomplishment from having pushed myself physically, quietly reflecting as I negotiated one step after the next.

This is a Red Bull wall, and also someone selling anti-monkey safety sticks.

After arriving back in Emei Town, I had a huge lunch and hopped on the high-speed rail for Chengdu, where I've spent the last few days resting, visiting museums, drinking tea, reading, and enjoying numbingly spicy hot pot.
