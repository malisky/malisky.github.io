October 8, 2025: Golden Week in Ningxia
October 8, 2025: Golden Week in Ningxia - Michael Alisky

Dear friends, family members, and former roommates,

It's 7am, and I'm about to arrive in Beijing. I spent the night on an overnight train from Ningxia, where I was lucky to even get a seat.

It's the tail-end of Golden Week, a holiday celebrating the October 1949 founding of the People's Republic of China, or as my Chinese textbooks call it, Liberation. The whole country gets the week off, so to say travel is hectic would be an understatement. Tickets vanish within seconds of release, hotels triple their prices, and in popular tourist destinations like Shanghai and Hangzhou, one need not even walk — the faceless crowds will simply carry you from site to site. On my train, the aisles and areas between cars were packed with less fortunate travelers who had to stand for the entire 11-hour journey, shifting their weight from foot to foot as the train crawled through the darkness.

Ningxia is a small province in Northwest China, somewhat evocative of America's midwest. It's a cradle of Chinese civilization, with archaelogical records of stone carvings that go back over 25,000 years. In addition to coal mining and corn production, it's also got some great hiking. I visited with Maya, Kristina, and Barberine, three friends from my Chinese language program in Beijing.

Ningxia became an autonomous region in 1958, carved out of Gansu province as a homeland for the Hui people, a Muslim minority group who wear distinctive white caps. When Mao Zedong led the communists through the Liupan mountains in southern Ningxia in October 1935, fleeing the Guomindang on the Long March, he wrote:

The sky is high, the clouds are pale. We watch the wild geese vanish to the south. If we cannot reach the Great Wall we are not true men...

Historically, Ningxia has been a region wracked by famine and poverty, particularly Xihaigu in the south, which was once called the most unfit place for human settlement by the United Nations World Food Programme. Over the past thirty years, Xihaigu has been the target of a major (and largely succesful) poverty alleviation campaign.

Our first stop though was Yinchuan, a city of 2.8 million people, which is about the size of Chicago. We took advantage of a robust rentable electric moped system and puttered down eerily empty streets at a maximum speed of 10 miles per hour, blasting music from the Baby Driver soundtrack through my phone's tinny speakers.

In Haibao Park, we munched on carbuncular watermelons as a nearby old man played with his drone (打飞机). A little kid (xiao pengyou) pointed at us and yelled English! before his mom slapped him, and another xiao pengyou came up to take a picture before asking if Black people were dangerous.

The further one ventures from Beijing, the more attention one attracts for being a foreigner. In Longde county outside of Guyuan, population 150,000, a sea-buckthorn-juice vendor told us we were the only foreigners she had ever seen, except for a marathon runner a few years back. Lips still puckering from the bracingly tart juice (it kind of reminds me of wet socks), we soon found ourselves ushered into a Shanxi-style lianpu opera performance, the local troupe smiling as deafening kuaibans and drums crashed on stage.

At the Western Xia tombs near Zhongwei, pyramid-shaped mausoleums rose from the earth like piles of coal, their edges softened by centuries of wind and their innards picked clean by looters.

The Western Xia Kingdom built its capital here in the lee of the Helan mountains and ruled from 1038 to 1227, when Genghis Khan's ferocious cavalry launched its first major incursion into China with the explicit goal of eradicating the entire Western Xia culture. In addition to the tombs, the Tangut script, which British Orientalist Gerard Clauson described as one of the most inconvenient of all scripts, remains a major relic of this civilization.

After leaving the tombs, we spent the night in the Shapotou desert in a fun but somewhat overstimulating glamp-site. They had karaoke, ATVs, military-style desert trucks, and at 9pm, all the xiao pengyou's gathered around the bonfire for a ritualistic dance lesson and firework show. We left before any anybody was sacrificed.

I also met this enormous Alaskan Malamute named Mantou and asked if it was a dog or a woof woof, having confused the Chinese for wolf (lang) and woof (wang).

In Guyuan, we were rained out of our visit to Liupan Shan, and as water cascaded down mountain roads shrouded in sheets of mist, our driver suggested we check out the nearby Long March memorial instead. There, a chain-smoking man wearing a military surplus jacket explained that George W. Bush (little Bush in Chinese) couldn't eat sashimi when he visited Tokyo as it had been poisoned by his Japanese hosts, while another man assured me that China could successfully invade Taiwan within two hours if it wanted to.

The combination of it being Golden Week, us being conspicuous foreigners, and the Long March memorial being a decidedly nationalistic touristic attraction made such conversations all but inevitable, not to mention the baseline urge of every Chinese uncle to explain history ad nauseum to strangers.

Also, here's a literal Capitalist roader and also a tank made out of yogurt containers.

I'm glad to have spent so much time outdoors and to have gotten to travel with new friends. The highlight of the trip was definitely the Sumeru Grottoes. Despite it being Golden Week, we had the place basically to ourselves. The main attraction is a 20-meter Tang Dynasty Maitreya Buddha, but I enjoyed the surrounding scenery more.

Small copses of pine trees dot the bare summits, and over 130 caves carved during the 5th through 10th centuries peer out from the red sandstone cliffs. Standing in the dimly-lit Cave 51 towers the ight of the Sumeru mountain. Looking up, I felt my breath catch. The combination of solitude, filtered darkness, and our hike through valley floors strewn with fallen rocks invited a real moment of peace.

I'm arriving in Beijing soon and it's straight to class. As always, thanks for reading.

好好学习，天天向上。

Until next time,
Michael
