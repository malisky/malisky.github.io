February 17, 2026: Cambodia

Ripping through the streets of Siem Reap on a semi-automatic motorcycle, my olfactory senses alerted me to the smell of grilled meat. I quickly pulled off the road and found myself in a Cham (one of Cambodia’s Muslim minorities) neighborhood.

I ordered chicken heart skewers from a roadside vendor, and as I sat down, a middle-aged man named Rashid told me I needed to get some rice too. He handed me a skewer with beef sausages. Try it! I took a small bite. Delicious!

You keep it! he exclaimed. I protested; he persisted. We talked for a while. Rashid patted his round, avuncular belly and hopped on his bike. Bye bye!

A short while later, an old woman named Maret sat down next to me. I stood up to grab her a spoon, and she beamed. Thank you very much! [in Khmer] She pointed to her grilled fish. Eat with me!

Later, Maret informed me that tonight was Happy New Year — China. I asked if she wanted any chicken. She grimaced and pointed to her knees. No chicken. Fish. I clicked my tongue disapprovingly. In broken Khmer, I quoted Book 3 of The Republic: You know that during the Trojan War, Homer at the heroes’ feasts does not entertain them with fish — even though they are on the seashore in the Hellespont — nor on boiled meat, but on grilled meat only (404b–c). Maret scoffed and waved her hand: Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; see that it is paid. (Phaedo 117a–118a). She unzipped a hidden pouch sewn directly into her floral shirt and pulled out a fat wad of cash. Bye bye!

My main reasons for coming to Cambodia were a) to escape Chinese New Year and b) to visit Angkor Wat, the enormous 12th-century Buddhist/Hindu religious complex located outside of Siem Reap. Also, if it wasn't obvious, that last bit about quoting Plato was a joke. Everything else is true though.

I explored the main ruins of Angkor Wat with Bat-Orgil, a friend who long-term readers may recognize from my Mongolia travelogue, and his friend Songka.

Outside the Bayon temple, we watched a monkey greedily enjoy a banana-flavored popsicle, sluttishly savoring each bite while looking around to make sure no one was coming to steal his trophy.

As we poked around the laterite ruins, guano drizzled onto the floor from unlit caverns above. In Russia, they say it’s good luck if a bird poops on you; I think if a bat poops on you, though, you should probably get tested for various airborne diseases.

I paid special attention to Angkor Wat’s hydraulic systems, which include river intake channels, distribution channels, and drainage reservoirs called barays. While the decline of the Khmer empire is often attributed to the Ayutthaya invasion in 1431, a more recent body of scholarship has emphasized hydraulic collapse brought about by ecological degradation and large-scale drought (see Buckley 2010 and Fletcher et al. 2008).

One evening, we took a boat up the Tonlé Sap, Southeast Asia’s largest freshwater lake. The lake normally drains south into the Mekong, but during monsoon season, water levels rise dramatically, causing the Mekong to reverse flow and pushing a huge bounty of fish back into the lake. Teenagers playing volleyball and blasting music between stilt houses waved from the shore. We caught the sunset from a floating restaurant with an on-site crocodile farm.

I spent the week prior in Kampot and Kep in the south, renting a motorcycle for $5 a day and eating fried crabs on the beach. I spent a day on Mount Bokor and another near the border with Vietnam. Here are some shots from the road:

In Sihanoukville a few hours away, I loitered outside the Jin Bei casino, a major site of online scam operations. Amnesty International’s 2025 report details widespread human rights abuses including slavery, human trafficking, child labor, and torture at 53 locations like this one across the country. Cyber scams in Cambodia generate an estimated $12.5 billion a year, equivalent to half the country’s GDP.

According to the US Department of Justice, Chen Zhi, a Fujian-born businessman, was “the mastermind behind [this] sprawling cyberfraud empire.” Chen obtained Cambodian citizenship in 2014 and founded Prince Group, a corporate conglomerate that soon became “one of Asia’s largest transnational criminal organizations.”

Until recently, the Cambodian government had protected Chen, who worked as a personal advisor to the then–Prime Minister; earned a royal title equivalent to “lord”; and was named three-time Entrepreneur of the Year in Cambodia. However, mounting pressure from recent border clashes with Thailand, a travel ban from South Korea, sanctions from the US, and asset seizures by Singapore and the United Kingdom, led the Cambodian government to give Chen up. In January 2026, Chen was arrested and extradited without trial to China.

Signage at the Jin Bei casino has been covered up, and a red banner over the door reads, interior renovations. At a Fujianese restaurant across the street, workers were reticent about the situation. I’ve come to find whenever I ask a Chinese person what they perceive to be a probing or sensitive question, there’s a good chance they’ll feign ignorance and answer modestly: I am just an ordinary citizen. I don’t understand these types of things!

A customer, though, from Nanjing told me that he heard scam operators had been tipped off in advance of police raids. I asked him what he thought would happen to Chen.

The government is much stricter in China than here.

China is a safe country, I responded euphemistically. He grunted approvingly.

Sihanoukville used to be a bucket-list destination for many backpackers, but as one woman named Leena told me, in recent years the city has become unvisitable. Large-scale Chinese money laundering has transformed the beachfront town into a concrete abomination of restaurants, casinos, and unfinished hotels. Most people now stop by only to catch a ferry to the islands of Koh Rong, which are undeniably beautiful.

In Phnom Penh, a guide named Ta took me across the Cambodia–Japan Friendship Bridge to visit fishermen who live on their boats by the Chroy Changvar peninsula. We stopped at Sampov Treileak temple, where an old woman was tending to the grounds. I tried speaking to her with Google Translate, but the app couldn’t parse what she was saying. She flashed all ten fingers, twice, then held up two. 102 years old? I asked. She smiled. I later found out from Ta that the woman was likely Vietnamese. Ta pointed to the nearby Sokha Phnom Penh Hotel, a five-star, 549-room tower overlooking the Tonlé Sap River. The hotel has been trying to buy the land the temple sits on but has not yet succeeded.

Ta also told me he believes many Vietnamese nationals are working inside the Cambodian government, a suspicion shared by many Cambodians. I asked what could be done to combat that. My king is gentle, he told me. He’s a gentleman, he corrected himself. No power.

The first place I visited upon arriving in Cambodia was the S-21 interrogation center and Choeung Ek Killing Fields. At execution sites across Cambodia, some 2.5 million people, a quarter of the population, perished at the hands of the Khmer Rouge between 1975 and 1979.

I sat for a while on a bench across from a stupa filled with excavated skulls as a memorial to the victims. A tourist took off his hat, put on his sunglasses, and posed for a selfie. He nudged the glass door open to get a better shot, and a worker selling lotus flowers ran over to stop him.

In the pub-lined streets of Siem Reap, European teenagers on gap years cavort past old men eager to pay for romance and sex, their arms draped around women half their age. Average wages in Cambodia sit between $200–$300 a month, the same price as my round-trip ticket from Beijing. Meanwhile, rapacious developers gobble up land as children wander street food markets begging for peel-tabs from beer cans.

I’ve had a lot of time alone this trip, yet I’ve struggled to make sense of what hasn’t been sitting right with me. Part of it is the language barrier and the feeling that interactions with locals are tinted by the pressure of a transaction and economic inequality. The impossibility of reconciling the recent historical trauma, ongoing exploitation, and transience of travel have also left me dizzy and uncomfortable.

At the same time, I’ve learned a lot, and I’ve had many peaceful moments, mostly riding on a motorbike, feeling the wind and sun; relaxing on the beach with a book; or wandering through dense, dipterocarp forests. Compared to China, it has been nice not to feel constantly surveilled but strange to feel so conspicuously like a tourist.

Most of all, I am grateful for the sheer beauty of Cambodia, and for getting to share food and laughs with people who were far too kind and far more curious than I would be in their shoes.

Thank you as always for reading, and Happy New Year — China.
