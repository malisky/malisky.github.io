March 14, 2026: A Few Days in Dongbei

Da Meng, a 23-year-old police officer in Anshan, set the basketball down and offered me a cigarette. Over the last few days, I had rejected every cigarette Da Meng handed me. Coming up with increasingly creative excuses to say no was becoming something of a game itself.

"That sign says 'no smoking'," I gestured to the wall.

"That's just a suggestion."

"It's bad for your health."

Da Meng lifted his shirt, revealing a set of washboard abs. "No problem!"

"What if someone asks me to play the suona?"1 I protested. "I'll need my lungs to be strong!"

Anshan is a tier-three city outside of Shenyang in northeastern China. The main attractions are the local Wanda mall, the Buddha-shaped Qianshan mountain, and the sprawling Angang Steel factory on the valley floor below, hidden beneath a layer of smog. I spent around a week in Anshan for the New Year, staying with my good friend Daniel and his family, including his cousin Da Meng.

According to an article from the People's Daily in 1960, the facilities at Angang once employed 100,000 workers and contributed 25% to 40% of the nation's total steel and iron output. Today, Angang employs just 30,000 people, and Dongbei plays a much more marginal role in China's overall economy.

Most everyone I talked to expressed pessimism about the future. Since the 1980s, the economy has slowed down dramatically, and jobs are harder to come by. Between 2010 and 2020, Dongbei's three provinces - Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang - lost more than 30% of their population. An old man making decorative carved eggshells outside Zhang Xueliang's mansion in Shenyang told me he hasn't been selling as many pieces as he used to.

Mingji, a 24-year-old math teacher I met playing basketball in Haicheng, told me he would go abroad if he could. I asked him why he became a teacher, and he said the job was stable. I asked why everybody drinks and smokes so much in Dongbei. He shrugged. "There's not much else to do."

Most mornings, I was woken by the sound of fireworks. Daniel's uncle dropped 500 RMB - about $70 - on munitions-grade pyrotechnics for us to use, and we set them off in a parking lot in front of a local hospital. I learned that the word cigarette can be used as a transitive verb, as in, go cigarette that firework.

The bigger, louder, and more noxious one's fireworks, the better.

Daniel's oldest aunt had us over one night for tieguo stewed goose, dumplings, fried silkworms, and fresh Nanguo pears.

The men sat together, and we drank beer while discussing international relations and Chinese history. Someone asked me to demonstrate the Jewish dance,2 which I declined, but I had to do something, so I recited Wang Han's The Song of Liangzhou, a borderlands poem from the Tang dynasty. I had learned it from my building's doorman, Jack:

葡萄美酒夜光杯，

Fine grape wine in a glowing jade cup,

欲饮琵琶马上催。

About to drink, the pipa3 player calls us to mount our horses.

醉卧沙场君莫笑，

Lying drunk on this sandy field, don't you laugh at me,

古来征战几人回？

Since ancient times, how many who left for war returned?

— Wang Han (王翰), "The Song of Liangzhou"

A few days later, I asked Da Meng if I could go on a ride-along with him, but he said his bosses would probably give him trouble. I asked what the biggest security issues were in Anshan, and he told me he mostly dealt with prostitution, fights, and drug use. He showed me photos from a work group chat where his colleagues had raided a gay massage parlor. Haughty police officers posed for photos with half-naked detainees.

Daniel's other cousin, Da Zhuang, asked if I had ever smoked marijuana. What's it like? Is it easy to find in the United States?

"It's quite common," I told him. "The government makes a lot of money from taxes on marijuana sales. Most people think it's pretty harmless, about the same as alcohol."

"How many guns does the average American own?" Da Meng asked. "Is it one per person, or one per family?"

"Everybody gets one gun when they enter high school," I told him. "And you can get a second one if you pass America's version of the gaokao."

Daniel asked if he ever had to beat anyone up, and Da Meng flashed a toothy grin. "I don't like to, but sometimes you need to show a little strength!"

By this point, it was well past midnight, and we got in Da Meng's Volvo and gallivanted around town, driving far too fast and blasting Kodak Black and Kendrick far too loud. Growing up in suburban Denver, this was one of the main things we did for fun when I was in high school, and it felt nostalgic and surreal to be doing the same thing in a random city in Dongbei.

A few days later, I said goodbye to Daniel's family and headed to Dalian. I walked around Zhongshan Square for a bit but found it rather boring, so I headed to the ferry dock and booked a ticket to Yantai. As I waited for the boat, I hung out with a nice and rather photogenic cat guarding the frozen seafood.

Almost every time I travel between cities in China, I end up giving the nearest xiao pengyou a free English lesson. This time, that happened to be Luning, a first grader named after the provinces his parents are from: Shandong (known in ancient times as Lu) and Liaoning. Luning's mom, Tianyuan, gave us sausages and crackers to feed the seagulls. Most of our snack volleys were snatched cleanly out of the air by the graceful and seafaring aviary rats, but a few missed, plopping into the Bohai Sea with an imperceptible splash. I was reminded of Jingwei, the daughter of a mythical emperor who drowned in this sea and was reincarnated as a bird. She spent eternity dropping pebbles and twigs into the East Sea in a futile but valiant attempt to fill it in. When nobody was looking, I surreptitiously wolfed down some sausage and cracker myself, too cheap to spend my dubloons on instant ramen at the onboard canteen.

When we arrived in Yantai seven hours later, Tianyuan insisted on giving me a ride to my hotel. Her husband, Guanhua, picked us up, grandmother and grandfather in tow. I complimented the vehicle, and the adults squeezed to make room. Shandong people are known for their hospitality, and I made a half-hearted effort at declining their invitation to dinner. Even though they had work the next morning, we talked for over two hours, gorging on fresh sea snails, beef skewers, and more beer. Luning ate roasted bread and watched videos on his iPad, and I learned a great deal about the city's electric grid management and local Shandong dialect.

I've been drawn to Dongbei for a while, mostly because of stories from some of my closest friends, and the pervasive sense of nostalgia and hardship and transformation. I was also inspired by one particularly irreverent meme account on Instagram. Today, Big Flower jackets, standup comedians, and TV shows like The Long Season, contribute to Dongbei's purported cultural renaissance. I hope to come back and spend more time here, and Shandong as well.

This is my first time publishing a travel story on Substack. Usually I just email them out to friends and family, which I think I'll continue to do, but I thought I'd also try something new. Let me know what you think!

Thanks for reading, and until next time,
