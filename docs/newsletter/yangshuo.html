January 30, 2026: Sojourn in Sichuan (and Chongqing and Yangshuo)

Happy New Year!

Greetings from Beijing! And now onto Sichuan...

At Chengdu’s Shufeng Yayun teahouse, a red-faced suona master blasted a pinwheeling melody over a catchy EDM backtrack, steam rising from his body as throngs of xiaopengyoumen bounced with excitement like popcorn in a Dutch oven. My mom — who was expecting something far different when I told her I had bought tickets for the opera — was, to my surprise, also enjoying the show.

In China, the suona is known as the king of the instruments. It's an exceptionally piercing musical implement, something like a clarinet on steroids, hotwired to a 50-watt amp. The suona master wiped his brow and took a bow before giving the stage to Pi Jin and his rolling light performance, followed by hand shadows and a face-changing show. Audience members sat around small tables with communal bowls of sunflower seeds. The opera house also offered free massages and ear cleaning.

Tourism in China is particularly well-suited for those afflicted by ADHD: there’s always another activity being advertised, always something else to catch your eye. For instance, in Yangshuo, Guangxi, as my little brother and I were riding electric mopeds through a karst-lined freeway when we spotted an enormous butterfly sculpture attached to the side of a cliff. We pulled over, bought tickets for $5, and began our climb through a cave filled with gaudy, multi-colored stalactites made of plaster and papier-mâché.

Getting past the on-site photographers was like navigating a route full of Pokémon trainers who all want to challenge you to a battle. We reached the top and were ushered toward a slideway and handed cotton gloves and camouflage pants with built-in ass-pads to reduce friction. We simply couldn’t turn that down, and as Peter and I sped down the slick stone chute, workers shouted at us to slow down.

At lunch, an old lady approached us and asked if we wanted a taxi to take us to a secret viewpoint for the magical Moon Mountain, which we soon found on our own just two minutes up the road. Someone else interrupted to show us his cormorants. ¥10 photo, he shouted.

These fishing birds aren’t actually native to Southern China; they’re brought in from Shandong in the east. As Friar Odoric, a Franciscan missionary from Friuli, Italy, described during his travels through China in the early 14th century, fisherman tie a thread around the cormorants’ necks to prevent them from swallowing the fish they catch (The Travels, Chapter IX). This BBC video from 2010 shows fisherman using the exact same technique some 700 years later.

In Xingping village, Peter and I ate beer-braised fish (unsure as if there was an avian middleman involved) and caught the sunset at the top of Laozhai mountain, its peak crowded with tourists jostling for a view. Recalling Wang Zhihuan’s famous lines in his poem, Dengguan Quelou (登鹳雀楼), we scrambled to the top of a precarious pile of rocks:

欲穷千里目，更上一层楼。

“Yearning to see the vast vista, one must ascend yet another level.”

— Wang Zhihuan (王之涣), On the Stork Tower

We chatted with a few high school students from nearby Guilin, who had been loudly strategizing about how best to ask us for a picture, shyly stealing glances in our direction.

A few weeks later, back in Chengdu, I drank white tea served over a small brazier with various fruits and nuts. The pigeons of Qingyang temple must have been exceptionally well-fed, or otherwise suspicious of foreigners, because they completely ignored the roasted mantou I tossed to them. One pigeon valiantly attempted a mating dance, but the object of his desire turned her head and flew away, leaving him alone to sulk.

My mom especially liked all the tea we drank in Sichuan, and also the red pandas at Dujiangyan, a suburb of Chengdu. I was more excited about the ancient irrigation system that redirected the Min River toward the Chengdu plain, built in 256 BC and still in use today. Dujiangyan enabled consistent irrigation (and therefore, stable surplus) in Western China, laying the foundation for the Qin dynasty’s development and the unification of China. Ironically, Dujiangyan had some of the worst bathrooms I’ve seen in China, the details of which I will spare my dear readers.

Having satisfied one of my main special interests (ancient hydraulics), we moved on to the next one (Daoism) and climbed Mount Qingcheng but found it too cloudy to see anything notable. The roasted sweet potatoes were delicious though.

In the Wulong Karst region, site of Transformers 4: Age of Extinction, I met this lovely group of ladies from Dongbei who were doing a photoshoot.

In Chongqing, I met the dental wizard of the Bayu metro, watched old men play cards, and suffered through brutally spicy hot pot with my friend Claire.

A small child dropped a Rubik’s cube, and as I picked it up to hand it to him, I asked what it was called in Chinese (answer: puzzle cube). He looked at me like I was an idiot and pointed to a sign behind him. We had, of course, stumbled onto Chongqing’s 6th Annual Youth Speed Cubing Competition, which I was unfortunately not allowed to register for.

The next day, Claire and I joined a geriatric Chinese tour group to visit Dazu and see the Buddhist rock sculptures of Mount Baoding. As we were bussed from attraction to attraction, I chatted with Old Man Zhang, who moved to Australia over forty years ago and now runs a Chinese restaurant, and who gave me several plump oranges and lots of life advice.

That night, an elaborate drone show took off over the muddy Yangzi River, and later, I made conversation with a few Russian students outside my hotel. This was back in October, and they wanted to know: what were my thoughts on Charlie Kirk? Is LGBT a real issue in America? What kind of music do I like? More specifically, what did I think about Lil Pump? What do Americans think about Russia — and what do Americans think about Russians?

I’ve had far more opportunities to speak Russian here in China than I was expecting. In December, I performed in a waltz with the Russian Speaking Student Association at Tsinghua’s Winter Gala, which ended up being a hugely rewarding endeavor. It's the first time in my life I've had a community of Russian people my age, and they've been warm, friendly, and curious (and incredibly non-politically-correct).

In Shanghai, I visited the Longhua temple with Marcelo and Fiona, where we were approached by an 84-year-old man named Wang who wanted to practice his English. Before long, we learned he had studied Russian during the Cultural Revolution, later became an English teacher, and could sing in Italian as well as Russian, in a powerful baritone. I joined him for a few Soviet-era songs like Катюша and Тёмная ночь (Dark Night).

In Leshan, I ate skewers and sweet-skin roast duck and met Francis from Singapore, who builds and operates hotels across China. He and his wife — she’s from Leshan — gave me a lift to the train station. On the way, she teased him: His Chinese is better than yours!

I had some tea at the Giant Buddha, a seventy-one meter-tall and exceptionally ugly stone statue erected between 713 and 803 AD. Across the river, apartments offer a full view of the holy toes. A monk near the bottom corrected me when I asked when he decided to become a monk. I didn’t decide. It was fate/predestined affinity. (緣分).

I also chatted with some security guards there. Did spending so much time at a place like this make them believers? Their answer: no.

Thank you so much for reading, and thank you to those who asked where these newsletters had gone and when the next one was coming. I’ve been in a monk era of my own, memorizing poems and learning classical Chinese, and it’s been far too long since I sat down and written something. I’ve collected lots of notes and observations from my travels that I still need to reflect on and synthesize, so expect more to come. Thank you also for the birthday wishes earlier this week.

Until next time,

Michael
