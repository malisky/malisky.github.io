April 22, 2026: Yunnan, part 1

Lijiang, Tiger Leaping Gorge, and Shangri-La.

It started to rain, which made the narrow walkway slippery and precarious. I looked at the languid Jinsha below, the gorge's towering limestone walls belying the river's true violence. The Jade Dragon Snow Mountain reached into the opaque clouds above.

The weather was clear in the morning. I had some tea with the hotel owner, Ali, and his brother, who dropped me off at Qiaotou village. I walked past a construction site and some cows grazing in a bucolic pasture.

At the Tiger Leaping Gorge trailhead, a police officer handed me a registration form. I wrote down my incredibly common Chinese name, 李明, and the phone number for a local pizza restaurant by my house, which I still remember on account of having relentlessly prank called them when I was 10 years old. I promised not to start any forest fires.

A few hundred meters past the entrance, I chatted with a Naxi woman selling roasted nuts and walking sticks. Naxi are one of the main indigenous ethnic groups in Yunnan, along with Bai, Yi, Tibetan and over twenty others. The woman told me she's 80 years old and still hikes the 22-kilometer trail regularly.

"80 years old‽" I exclaimed. "You don't look a day over 60!" She flashed a toothless grin. I bought some water and said goodbye.

I walked with some German tourists and shared some travel advice before bidding them a very Austrian Tschuss, Baba. After lunch, I fell into line with four hikers from Guizhou. The clouds knitted and unraveled, and the sun flickered and absconded. As we walked, I fielded questions about the war in Iran and gun violence in America, and we commiserated over the job search.

Meng offered me a cigarette, which I declined. A few hundred meters down trail, he gave it to a goat.

"You're going to hell," I told him, taking the lit cigarette from the goat's mouth. Meng howled with laughter.

I said goodbye to my new friends, who gave me a ride back to my hotel. They helped me arrange a ride to Shangri-La, a real city named after a fictional utopia from James Hilton's 1933 novel, Lost Horizon. In 2001, Yunnan province held a contest wherein multiple towns conducted research to prove they were the real Shangri-La. The capital of Diqing Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Zhongdian County, won, rebranding itself overnight and filling its streets with yak-butter creameries and monastery-cum-gift-shops.

I liked the Temple of One Hundred Chickens - I only saw the one, though. The name comes from a Buddhist practice called mercy release, in which devotees carry chickens here and set them free as a vow of blessing. I think that the releasing of cursorial birds from this height is a somewhat optimistic gesture. They can't exactly fly down.1

Sitting atop a wind-strewn hill, the temple offers a sweeping bird's-eye view of the Dukezong ancient town. When I say bird's eye view, I am of course referring to the view of a bird capable of sustained, graceful, high-altitude flight, not that of a chicken.2 As I left, I spotted a nervous forest cow peeking at me through the trees.

At the Songzanlin Monastery, I eavesdropped on a Tibetan tour guide lounging on a set of warm stone stairs. He explained to a group of tourists that in Tibetan, Shangri-La means sun and moon in the heart. The guide fidgeted with his pant-leg and propounded breathily: "Shangri-La is not a place. It's a mindset. A mindset that all travelers share."

I left the group and wandered around the monastery's outskirts. Founded in 1679 by the Fifth Dalai Lama, Songzanlin is the largest Tibetan Buddhist monastery in Yunnan. It was destroyed during the Cultural Revolution and rebuilt after 1981.

I sat in a grove of prayer flags, watching chickens perambulate on the stones. I was thinking about how a Tibetan region gradually and peacefully absorbed by China made the enormously profitable decision to adopt an imagined name coined by a British man who spoke neither Chinese nor Tibetan, creatively retrofitting a poetic and celestial etymology, when a Han Chinese woman wearing gaudy makeup and Tibetan-style clothes tapped me on the shoulder, snapping me out of my reverie. "Shuai ge," she smiled sweetly. "Could you move so I can take some photos?"

I got up, rolling my eyes and muttering impolite words in Russian. I wandered into another temple, but now I was irretrievably annoyed. Shangri-La was beautiful, but the whole place felt commercial and cold and camp, in a weird religious way. I changed my train ticket to leave for Dali a day earlier.

I think I'll end this newsletter here and save those travels for the next one (spoiler: Dali was awesome), but before I do, I'll share a few additional photos and anecdotes from Lijiang, which is the city I flew into from Beijing:

When I left the train station, I asked my corpulent, middle-aged taxi driver what kind of music he liked. He started blasting "What Makes You Beautiful" by One Direction. I asked if he liked any local music, and he put on a Bai song, which was so beautiful I did not even think to ask its name.

Another taxi driver asked if I'm married, and I told him I'm not, and he said he could help me find a local wife. Before I could respond, though, he warned me that the local women are no good, and launched into a rant about the types of women he disliked most.

Almost everywhere I went, people asked me if I knew Lin Deng, an American who moved to Yunnan 40 years ago and who speaks local Naxi dialect. I looked him up. His name is Brian Linden. He actually went to Stanford too.

One day, I walked about 25 kilometers from Lijiang to Yuhu Village, stopping for lunch in Baisha Ancient Town. In Yuhu, I wandered into a bookstore called 去你的书吧, which is run by a local poet.

At a hostel, I met a German guy named Max, who is bike-packing through Yunnan, currently on his way to Xining in Qinghai province. Over a beer, he showed me his YouTube page and told me stories about being detained in Afghanistan, Northern Iraq, and Iran.

Yunnan is famous for its mushrooms and berries, and even though it's not the right season, I really wanted to forage.

That's all for now. Thank you as always for reading. I had a great time in Yunnan, which really does live up to the hype. It's open, diverse, clean, and beautiful. I hope to go back soon.

For now though, I'm back in Beijing, where I'll stay until June. After that, I'll be in the Bay Area briefly. And who knows after that. Please message me if you'll be in town.

All the best,
Michael
